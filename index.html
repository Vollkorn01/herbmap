    About this map

    Each herb's likelihood is estimated for every ~50 m spot in Switzerland by combining two things:

    1. Habitat suitability — does the spot match where the herb grows? Each herb has an ecological profile (habitat types, altitude, moisture, light, lime or acidic soil, nutrients, warm slopes, and closeness to streams, forest edges, hedges, farms or paths). This is compared with official Swiss geodata:
      • Habitat Map of Switzerland (WSL/BAFU): 80+ habitat types such as hay meadows, dry grassland, floodplain forest or fens, plus hedges and single trees
      • swissALTIRegio (swisstopo): altitude, slope and sun exposure
      • swissTLM3D (swisstopo): streams, lakes, buildings, roads and paths
      • National Forest Inventory (WSL/BAFU): broadleaf vs conifer share
      • Soil suitability map (BLW): waterlogging, water and nutrient storage
      • Geotechnical map (swisstopo) and Geochemical Soil Atlas (BAFU): limestone vs acidic rock
    2. Real plant records — how common the herb is in the region (~5 km), from records since 1990 on GBIF (including Info Flora). The share of all plant records that are this herb corrects for some areas simply being surveyed more.
    • High (60–100) — good habitat in an area where the herb is common.
    • Medium (30–59) — worth a look.
    • Low (under 30) — possible, but don't expect much.

    The habitat profiles are expert rules, not a statistical model fitted to sightings — click any spot to see exactly why it scores as it does. Map data can be out of date or wrong at a single spot; use it as a guide for where to look.

    Safety

    This site is for education only and is not medical advice. Herbs can cause allergies, side effects and serious interactions with medication. Talk to a doctor or pharmacist before using herbs medicinally, especially if you are pregnant, breastfeeding, taking medication or treating a child.

    Never eat a wild plant unless you are 100% sure of its identity — several herbs have deadly lookalikes (e.g. wild garlic and lily of the valley, gentian and white false hellebore). Respect nature reserves and cantonal plant-protection rules.

    Evidence levels

    • Traditional use — long history of use, little clinical research.
    • Some evidence — some clinical studies, often small or mixed.
    • Good evidence — several well-designed clinical trials.
